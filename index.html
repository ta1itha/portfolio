// about

The Stack

Some people follow a straight path. I've been pushing commits.

Developer → educator of 700+ students → product manager → business analyst → student success manager. Each version shipped new thinking. Each role taught me how users, systems, and businesses actually work from the inside out — and the through-line, the part I keep coming back to, is the relationship with the person on the other side of the product.

I started in engineering, where I shipped iOS, Android, and web features at a fintech startup and abstracted 90% of duplicated code into a reusable package at a legal-tech firm. Then I spent three years teaching front-end development — which, stripped of the title, is just customer success at scale: onboarding, adoption, escalations, retention, and the LMS iteration that made all of it work better.

Now I sit at the intersection I was always moving towards: technical enough to talk to engineers, empathetic enough to understand the user on a 3G phone in shift work, and analytical enough to build a business case with ROI a CFO will actually believe.

I also have a strong conviction: if you're not doing anything with AI, you're choosing to be slower. In my current role as Student Success Manager, I'm using PowerAutomate and Claude Code to automate the onboarding admin that used to eat my customer-facing time — because every hour won back from repetitive process is an hour I get to spend with the people who actually need me.

Product Management Internship

Product Strategy · Agile · Process Improvement

The Product Was the Internship Itself

Most interns execute someone else's roadmap. At Stroop Digital, the internship is the product and you are the PM. The mandate: follow a structured agile learning pathway, ship real PM deliverables, and apply product strategy to improve the internship process for every cohort that comes after you. For a developer who has spent years refactoring other people's inefficient systems, this was a natural fit.

→ Self-managed delivery owned the full PM lifecycle end-to-end: discovery, prioritisation, roadmap, execution, and retrospective with no one assigning tickets.
→ Learning pathway as backlog treated each module (Business Canvas, OKRs, Customer Journey, MVP Design) as a sprint deliverable, tracking progress and blockers independently.
→ Process improvement output applied product strategy to identify friction points in the internship experience and proposed improvements to the programme for future interns.
→ Developer instinct, PM lens brought a builder's bias for optimisation: if something was slow, unclear, or repeated I documented it, reasoned about it, and proposed a better version.
